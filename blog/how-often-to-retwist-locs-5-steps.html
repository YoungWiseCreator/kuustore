How Often to Retwist Locs: 5 Steps, 1 DIY Check to Stop Thin Roots for You in 2026

Byline: Salon Guide, KuuStore — Published: 2026-11-27 — Last reviewed: 2026-11-27

She is in the chair in Miramar for her third retwist in six weeks, and her loctician — the stylist who cares for locs — has gone quiet in the way that means something. Locs are ropes of hair that form when strands are left to mat and hold together over time. Hers are eight months old, still young, still soft at the base. She loves the fresh look. She books the moment the roots puff up, every two weeks, because a neat head feels like a cared-for head. Her loctician parts the hair at her hairline, holds one loc up to the light, and shows her the base of it. The loc itself is thick and healthy. The root it hangs from is not. The base has gone narrow, like a heavy picture hung on a thin nail. You can see scalp through the roots along her edges where you could not see it in the spring. No one yanked anything. No one did anything wild. She just came back too soon, too often, with the best of reasons — she wanted it to look right. This post is about the clock she could not see: how often to retwist, stage by stage, so the neat look never costs you the roots it hangs from.

The short answer:

Retwist locs on the stage clock, not the mirror clock, in 5 steps: name your stage, use that stage's gap, judge by the root and not by the puff, keep the wash and the retwist as two different dates, and stretch the gap at the first sign of thin roots. The gaps most stylists work by run like this. Starter locs, in their first four to six weeks, may be retwisted every two to three weeks, then about every four weeks. Budding and teen locs do well every four to six weeks, and teen locs can stretch to eight. Mature locs often need a retwist only every eight to twelve weeks, and some go longer. A retwist is the act of twisting the new growth at the root back into the loc's shape. New growth is the soft, loose hair that grows out of the scalp between visits. Twist too often and the base of each loc gets worked before it has grown strong, and over months the root thins. Twist on the stage clock and the root stays thick enough to carry the loc for years. Run the 1 DIY check at the end before you book, and you will know if your next visit should be soon — or should be moved back.

Why does neat feel so urgent, and why does the root pay for it?

New growth is soft, loose, and puffy. It does not match the tight, rope-like loc below it, and the eye reads that mismatch as mess. So the mirror says: book now. But the root is not a style choice. The root is the base — the small bundle of strands at the scalp that holds the whole weight of the loc. Every retwist works that base: it gathers the loose growth and twists it down tight into the loc's shape. Done at the right gap, that is training. Done too soon, the same base gets gathered and twisted again before it has grown out enough new hair to be strong, and the twist pulls on the same strands at the same spot, over and over.

Think of it like a young tree tied to a stake. The tie helps the tree grow straight. But if you re-tie it tighter every week, cinching the same spot on a trunk that has not yet thickened, the tie starts to cut in. The tree does not need more tying. It needs time to grow bark and girth between tyings. A loc root is that trunk. Hair grows about half an inch a month for most people — that is the pace your calendar has to respect. A retwist every two weeks is asking the base to be worked twice for every half inch it grows. The math does not leave the root anything to thicken with. That is the loop: the neater you keep it week to week, the thinner the base gets month to month, until one day the neatest head in the room is also the one losing locs at the edges.

Step 1: What stage are your locs in right now?

Everything hangs on the stage, so name it first. Stylists tend to speak of five stages. The starter stage is the first three to six months, when the locs are new, soft, and still learning to hold. The budding stage runs roughly from six to twelve months, when the hair inside the loc starts to swell and pack down — budding is that first thickening you can feel with your fingers. The teen stage, around twelve to eighteen months, is when locs take on their real shape but can still act wild, puffing and shrinking. The mature stage comes after that, when the loc is packed tight along its whole length. The rooted stage is the long, settled stage after about two years, when the locs are fully formed and mostly just grow.

If you are not sure of your stage, count months since your start date, and then ask your stylist to confirm it at your next visit — stage is a thing a trained eye can call in seconds by touch. Write the stage down. It is the first line of every timing choice that follows.

Step 2: What gap does your stage ask for?

Here is the stage clock, the way working stylists lay it out. Starter locs in their first four to six weeks may be retwisted every two to three weeks, because young locs can slip and unravel, and early visits hold the shape while the hair learns. After that first stretch, starters settle to about every four weeks. Budding locs do well every four to six weeks. Teen locs do well every four to six weeks too, and many can stretch to eight as they firm up. Mature locs need far less: every eight to twelve weeks is common, and rooted locs can go longer still, with a retwist done mostly to tidy the look rather than to hold the shape.

Notice what the clock is really saying. The younger the loc, the more the visits are about holding shape. The older the loc, the more the visits are about looks alone — and looks can always wait a week or two without any harm done. The harm runs the other way. There is no stage on this clock where a standing every-two-weeks retwist is the plan, past the first month or so of a starter set. If that is your calendar, the calendar is the problem, not your hair.

Step 3: How do you judge by the root instead of the puff?

Between visits, learn to read the root the way your loctician does. Part the hair and look at the base of a few locs, including the ones at your hairline. Ask three things. Is there enough new growth to work with — a soft band of loose hair at the base that a twist can truly gather? Are the parts still clear, or are the roots of next-door locs starting to marry, which means to creep together and mat into one? And how does the base feel between your fingers — as thick as the loc below it, or narrow?

Puff alone is not a booking sign. Puff is just growth showing, and growth showing is the goal of the whole journey. Marrying roots are a booking sign, because two locs left to mat together must later be pulled apart, and pulling apart costs hair. A base that feels narrow is the opposite sign: it says move the visit back, not up. The turn in most loc care is right here — the moment you stop reading the mirror's neatness scale and start reading the root's strength scale. They rarely ask for the same date, and when they fight, the root wins.

Step 4: Why are wash day and retwist day two different dates?

Many people fuse the two: every wash ends in a retwist, so the locs get twisted as often as they get washed. That fusion is how a two-week twist cycle sneaks in through the back door. Keep them apart. Wash on the rhythm your scalp asks for — many loc wearers wash every one to two weeks, and active people who sweat a lot may wash more — and simply let the roots be puffy after most washes. A wash cleans the scalp and the loc. A retwist shapes the root. Clean is a weekly need. Shape is a monthly one.

Between retwists, you still have hands-on care to do, and it is the care that keeps the stage clock safe: separate the roots with your fingers after a wash, pulling apart any locs that are starting to creep together at the base, and palm roll — rolling a loc between your palms to keep its shape — when a loc feels loose. That light work, done weekly, is what lets you stretch the gap without the roots marrying. It takes minutes, it costs the base nothing, and it is the difference between a six-week gap that works and a six-week gap that ends in a painful separation session.

Step 5: What do you do at the first sign of thin roots?

Slow down, and say so out loud to your stylist. The signs are plain once you know them: bases that feel narrower than the loc, soreness or tenderness at the root that lasts more than a day after a retwist, small bumps around the base of a loc, a hairline that looks like it is creeping back, or a loc that feels weak where it meets the scalp. Any one of these means the next retwist moves back by weeks, not days, and the twist itself should be done with a lighter hand — edges and thin locs get about half the tension of the rest, a rule good stylists already work by.

Here is the cost beat, and it is the heart of this post. A skipped retwist costs you a few weeks of puff, which a scarf, a style, or plain patience covers. A root thinned past its limit can cost the loc itself, because a base that can no longer carry the loc's weight will break, and repeated pulling at the hairline can lead to a kind of hair loss from tension that may not fully grow back if it goes on too long. Neat is cheap to lose and easy to get back. Roots are neither. When the two ask for different dates, pay for the roots.

One honest limit belongs here. This post can teach you the clock and the signs, but it cannot see your scalp. If you have pain, heavy flaking, sores, or a thin spot that keeps widening, that is past a timing question — get seen in person, by your loctician first and by a skin doctor if the signs do not settle, because scalp problems caught early are the ones that turn around.

What is the 1 DIY check to run before you book?

Do the pinch test at your mirror, on three locs: one at the hairline, one at the crown, which is the top of the head, and one at the back. For each, pinch the base gently between your thumb and finger, then slide down and pinch the loc itself about an inch lower. Compare the two pinches. If the base feels close in thickness to the loc, your roots are carrying their load, and you can book on your stage's normal clock. If the base feels clearly thinner — a narrow neck with a heavy loc under it — do not book a retwist. Book a talk instead: push the twist back at least a few weeks, keep washing and separating on your weekly rhythm, and let your stylist feel those bases in person before any twisting happens. Three locs, two pinches each, one honest answer. That is the check.

The bottom line

How often to retwist locs is not a fixed number. It is a clock set by your stage: two to three weeks only in the first weeks of a starter set, about four weeks for young locs, four to six through the budding and teen stages, and eight to twelve or more once your locs are mature. Judge by the root, not the puff. Wash often, twist rarely, separate weekly, and stretch the gap at the first narrow base. Do that, and the woman in the chair keeps both things she came in with — the neat look she loves and the strong roots it hangs from.
