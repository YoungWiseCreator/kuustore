How to Prep Hair for Braids: 5 Steps, 1 DIY Check to Stop the Itch for You in 2026

Byline: Salon Guide, KuuStore — Published: 2026-11-21 — Last reviewed: 2026-11-21

Your braid day is set. You have the style saved on your phone. But the night before, a real question hits you: what do I do to my own hair first? Do you wash it? Do you oil it? Do you blow it out? If you get prep wrong, you can sit for hours with an itchy scalp, sore roots, and braids that look old in two weeks. If you get prep right, the same braids sit calm, feel light, and last the way they should. This post walks you through the 5 steps, in order, with the why for each one. It is the same prep we ask for at KuuStore in South Florida, where heat and damp air make a clean, dry start matter even more.

The short answer:

Prep your hair for braids in 5 steps: trim bad ends if you need to, wash your scalp and hair clean, deep condition to put water back in, add light moisture and work out all the knots, then stretch your hair and let it dry all the way. Do the wash the day or night before your visit, not the same hour. Come with hair that is clean, soft, knot-free, stretched, and fully dry, with no heavy grease on it. That one start stops most itch, most flakes, and most early frizz.

What the video shows: The video walks through a full braid prep from start to end. You see a clarifying shampoo used to wash the scalp clean, a deep mask put on to give the hair water back, and a wide-tooth comb used to work out knots while the hair is wet and soft. You see heat spray go on before a blow dryer with a comb tool stretches the hair out smooth. You also see ends checked and trimmed, and leave-in cream and a light oil worked in at the end. Watch the order: wash first, water next, knots out while wet, stretch last. That order is the whole lesson, and it is the same order this post teaches step by step.

Why does prep matter so much for braids?

Braids lock your own hair away for weeks at a time. Most sets stay in for about 6 to 8 weeks. In those weeks you can wash your scalp, but you can not wash and comb your own hair the way you do when it is loose. So the state of your hair on day one is the state it lives in for a long time.

Think of it like packing a bag for a long trip. If you pack clean, dry, folded clothes, the bag stays fresh for weeks. If you pack damp, dirty clothes and shut the bag, the smell and the mess grow in the dark. Your braids are that bag. Clean, dry, soft hair goes in, and it stays in good shape. Dirty, damp, knotted hair goes in, and the itch and flakes grow where you can not reach them.

Prep does three jobs at once. First, it cleans the scalp, so old oil, sweat, and product do not sit under the braids and itch. Second, it puts water deep into your hair, so your hair does not dry out and snap while it is tucked away. Third, it takes out knots and stretches the hair, so your stylist can part clean lines and braid with less pull. Less pull means less pain on day one and less breakage when the braids come out.

Step 1: Should you trim your ends before braids?

Yes, if your ends are split or thin, trim them before your braid day. A split end is the tip of a hair strand that has torn into two or more thin parts. Think of the end of a rope that has frayed. A frayed rope end keeps tearing up the rope. A split end does the same thing: the split can creep up the strand while the hair sits in braids.

Braids can not fix a split end. They hide it for weeks, and in those weeks the split can grow. When the braids come out, you may find you need a bigger cut than the small trim you skipped. A small trim now saves length later. That sounds backwards, but it is true: cut a little now so you do not have to cut a lot later.

You do not need a big cut. You need the dead, thin tips gone. Check your ends in good light. If the last bit of each strand looks see-through, rough, or split like a tiny fork, that bit should go. If your ends feel smooth and look full, you can skip this step.

Do the trim about 1 to 2 weeks before braid day if you can. That gap lets you see your hair in its true state after a wash or two. If you are not sure, ask your stylist to check your ends at the start of your visit and trim first if they need it. A good stylist would rather trim for 10 minutes than braid over ends that will break.

Step 2: How do you wash your hair the right way before braids?

Wash day is the heart of prep. Your goal is a scalp with no old product on it and hair that is clean but not stripped dry. Here is the wash, step by step.

First, take out knots before any water touches your hair. Work in 4 to 6 parts. Use your fingers first, then a wide-tooth comb, which is a comb with big gaps between its teeth. Start at the ends and move up in small bits. Dry hair with no product slip can snap if you rush, so go slow and be gentle. Knots left in now turn into hard tangles in the wash.

Next, wet your hair all the way through with warm water. Warm, not hot. Hot water can dry your scalp and hair out. Let the water run for a full minute or two so every strand is wet to the core.

Then shampoo. A clarifying shampoo is a strong shampoo made to lift off old oil, sweat, and product build-up. Use it for this wash if your hair holds a lot of product or feels coated. Put the shampoo on your scalp, not just on the lengths. Work it in with the soft pads of your fingers, never your nails. Nails can scratch the scalp, and scratched skin under fresh braids itches and flakes. Make small circles all over your head: front, sides, back, and the nape of your neck, which is the low back part of your head where sweat and product hide.

Rinse well. Then shampoo a second time if the first lather was thin or your scalp still feels coated. The second wash often foams more, and that foam is a sign the old build-up is gone. Rinse until the water runs clear and your hair feels clean, not squeaky and rough. Clean is the goal. Squeaky means you took too much oil out, and dry hair breaks more in braids.

Why does this step matter so much in South Florida? Heat and damp air make you sweat, and sweat plus old product on a scalp that can not breathe well is the top cause of braid itch. A true clean start is your best itch stop. That is the DIY check in the title: if your scalp feels clean and calm the night before, with no white flakes when you scratch lightly with a fingertip pad, your wash did its job.

Step 3: How do you deep condition so your hair stays soft in braids?

Shampoo takes oil and dirt out. Deep conditioning puts water back in. A deep conditioner is a thick cream that sits on the hair for a set time, often 15 to 30 minutes, so water and softeners can sink in. Hair that goes into braids dry will keep drying for weeks. Hair that goes in full of water holds up far better.

Put the deep conditioner on wet hair, part by part. Coat each part from root to tip, and give the ends extra. The ends are the oldest part of your hair and the part most likely to snap. Work the cream in with your fingers so no strand is left dry.

Cover your hair with a plastic cap. The cap holds in your body heat, and warm hair takes in cream better than cold hair. Leave it on for the time the jar says, often 15 to 30 minutes. Then rinse with cool water. Cool water helps the outer layer of the strand lie flat, and flat strands tangle less.

Think of your hair like a sponge. A dry sponge is stiff and tears if you bend it. A wet sponge bends and springs back. Deep conditioning soaks the sponge before it gets folded into braids for weeks. Fold a wet sponge and it is fine. Fold a dry one and it cracks. That is why this step is not a nice extra. It is the step that keeps your own hair safe while it rests.

Here is the same idea a second way, since this is the step most people skip. Think of a house plant you will not water for 6 weeks. You would soak the soil deep the day you leave, not sprinkle the top. Deep conditioning is that deep soak. A light spray on top does not reach the roots of the hair's needs. The long sit in thick cream does.

If your hair feels very weak or stretchy when wet, like soft gum, tell your stylist before braid day. Stretchy, mushy hair can be a sign it needs protein, which is a treatment that helps hair feel strong again. That call is best made by a pro who can feel your hair. Do not guess with strong treatments at home the night before.

Step 4: How do you add moisture and take out every knot?

After you rinse, your hair is clean and wet. Now you lock in the water and take out the last knots. This is the step where gentle hands save you the most hair.

Add a leave-in conditioner, which is a light cream or spray you do not rinse out. It keeps water in the strand and gives the comb slip, which means the comb can glide instead of drag. Put it on part by part. Then add a small amount of light oil or cream on top if your hair drinks up moisture fast. Small is the key word. Heavy grease is not moisture. Grease just coats the hair, and a thick coat can make parts look dull and can trap dirt at the root once the braids are in.

Now detangle, which means working out all the knots. Keep your 4 to 6 parts. For each part, hold the hair near the root with one hand so the pull does not hit your scalp. With the other hand, comb from the very ends up, one small bit at a time. When you hit a knot, stop. Work it loose with your fingers first. Never rip a comb through a knot. Ripping tears strands, and torn strands become the short, fuzzy bits you see when braids come out.

Take your time. For thick, long hair, this step can take 30 minutes or more. That is fine. Every knot you take out at home is a knot your stylist does not have to fight in the chair, and chair fights with knots mean more pull, more time, and more breakage.

When each part is smooth, twist it or clip it so it stays apart from the rest. By the end, you should be able to run your fingers through each part from root to tip with no stops. Run that check on every part. If a finger run stops, that part is not done.

Step 5: How do you stretch your hair and dry it all the way?

Stretched hair is hair that has been gently pulled straight or smooth while it dries, so it is not in tight, shrunken curls. Most stylists braid stretched hair faster and with cleaner parts. Stretching also shows your true length, so parts come out even.

You have two main ways to stretch. The first is heat: a blow dryer with a comb tool, used on warm heat with a heat protectant spray first. A heat protectant is a spray that coats the hair to cut heat harm. Work part by part, and keep the dryer moving. The second way is no heat: braid or twist each part into a few big braids or twists and let them dry that way, or wrap the hair in bands down its length. No-heat stretching takes longer, often overnight, but it is the gentlest path.

Whichever way you pick, one rule rules this step: your hair must be dry all the way through before braids go in. Damp hair shut inside braids can smell sour after a week or two, and a damp scalp itches. In Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach heat, thick hair can feel dry on the outside and still be wet at the roots. Check the roots with your fingers. If they feel cool and damp, give the hair more time.

Think of it like paint on a wall. You do not hang a picture on wet paint. You wait until the wall is dry to the touch all over. Hair is the same. Braids are the picture. Dry hair is the dry wall. Skip the wait, and the whole job suffers.

Ask your stylist how they want your hair to arrive. Some stylists wash and stretch hair at the salon as part of the visit. Some ask you to come washed and stretched. KuuStore visits are by appointment only, so a quick message before your day saves you doing work the salon would have done, or missing work the salon needs done. Ask these three things: Should I come washed? Should I come stretched? Should I put any product in, or come with none? The answers take one minute and set your whole prep.

Checklist:

  • Trim split or thin ends 1 to 2 weeks before braid day, if they need it
  • Detangle dry hair in 4 to 6 parts, ends first, before any water
  • Wash twice if you need to, with the pads of your fingers, never your nails
  • Deep condition for 15 to 30 minutes under a plastic cap, then rinse cool
  • Add leave-in, then detangle wet hair part by part until fingers glide through
  • Stretch each part with warm heat and protectant spray, or with big twists and no heat
  • Check the roots: hair must feel dry all the way through, not cool or damp
  • Message your stylist: come washed, stretched, with product or with none?

What should you not do the night before braids?

Some moves feel helpful and do harm. Keep this list in mind.

Do not put heavy grease, thick butter, or lots of gel on your hair after your wash. A heavy coat makes parts slippery in the wrong way, dulls the braids fast, and gives dirt a place to stick at the root. Light leave-in and a small bit of light oil are enough.

Do not wash and then sleep on loose, wet hair. You will wake up with a matted, tangled mass, and all your detangle work is lost. If you wash at night, stretch and tie your hair down with a silk or satin scarf before bed. Silk and satin are smooth fabrics that let hair glide instead of rub, so you lose less moisture and get less frizz.

Do not try a new product for the first time on prep night. A new cream or oil can make your scalp itch or flake, and you will not know if the itch came from the product or the braids. Use products your scalp already knows and likes.

Do not braid over a sore, broken, or very flaky scalp. If your scalp has open sores, bad pain, or thick scales that do not lift with a wash, see a doctor or a skin doctor first. Braids pull on the scalp, and a sick scalp needs care, not tension. This is an honest limit: prep fixes a dirty scalp, not a sick one.

Do not rush. Prep done in a panic, with ripping combs and damp roots, costs you hair. Give wash day its own block of time, the day or night before. Your future self, sitting calm in the chair, will thank you.

How does South Florida weather change your prep?

KuuStore serves Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach, and our air is warm and wet most of the year. That weather shapes prep in three ways.

First, sweat. You sweat more here, and sweat dries into salt and oil on the scalp. That is one more reason the wash must be a true scalp wash, not a quick rinse of the lengths. Clean the skin, not just the hair.

Second, drying time. Damp air slows drying. Hair that would dry in 2 hours in dry air can take far longer here. Start your stretch early, and check roots, not just ends, before you call it dry. If you wash the night before, your hair gets the full night to dry, which is a safe plan in our climate.

Third, frizz. Wet air puffs up hair that is not tied down. After you stretch, keep your parts twisted or clipped and your head covered with a satin scarf until your visit. None of this needs special products. It needs time and care.

What if you are prepping a tender head?

Tender-headed means the scalp feels pain fast when hair is pulled. Kids are often tender-headed, and so are many adults. Wash in parts so no step pulls the whole head at once. Detangle with lots of leave-in slip, in small bits. Stretch with no heat if heat is a problem: big twists overnight stretch hair with zero pain. On braid day, tell the stylist up front that the head is tender. Braids should feel snug, not like a headache. If a braid hurts bad enough to make eyes water, say so at once. Pain on day one is not a price you must pay for the style.
