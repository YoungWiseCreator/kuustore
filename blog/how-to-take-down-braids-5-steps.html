How to Take Down Braids: 5 Steps, 1 DIY Check to Stop Hair Loss for You in 2026

Byline: Salon Guide, KuuStore — Published: 2026-11-27 — Last reviewed: 2026-11-27

She is at her own bathroom mirror in Miramar on a Sunday night, and the trash bag next to her is full of cut braid hair. Her braids have been in for seven weeks. They looked great for six of them. Now her edges itch, the roots feel tight, and she has a work week that starts at 8 a.m. So she is doing what most of us do. She is pulling. One braid fights back at the root, where weeks of shed hair and product have packed into a small, hard knot. She tugs harder. The knot pops loose, and with it comes a clump that does not look like shed hair. It looks like her hair. Short pieces. Broken pieces. She stops, scissors in her hand, and stares at the one spot she did not want to see: a thin patch right at her hairline, the place every photo will show. The install took a full day and cost real money. The takedown is taking one tired hour, and it is undoing the whole point of the style. Braids are a protective style, which means a style that keeps your own hair tucked away so it can rest and grow. But the style only protects if the way out is as gentle as the way in. This post walks the way out, step by step, in plain words, so the length you grew stays on your head.

The short answer:

Take down braids in 5 steps: set aside real time and lay out your tools first, cut each braid below the point where your own hair ends, wet each braid with a water and conditioner mix so it has slip, unravel one braid at a time from the end up and slide the shed hair out with your fingers before any comb touches it, then wash in sections, deep condition, and let your hair rest before the next style. Most of the hair that comes out is shed hair, which is hair that left the root on its own during the weeks you wore the style and had nowhere to fall. A person sheds roughly 50 to 100 hairs a day in the normal course of things, so after six to eight weeks in braids, a big soft pile is expected and is not loss. Loss looks different: short broken bits, sore spots, and thin edges. Run the 1 DIY check at the end, and you will know which pile you are looking at before you panic.

If your own takedown feels like a fight, this video is the picture of what it should feel like instead — a calm job with a clear order, done one piece at a time.

Why is the takedown the part that decides if the style worked?

Here is the thing most people get wrong. They judge a protective style by how it looked in week two. The real test is week seven, at the mirror, with the scissors. Your hair grew the whole time it was braided. New growth pushed out of the root, and old hair shed the way hair sheds every day, braids or no braids. In loose hair, shed strands fall in the brush, in the shower, on the pillow. In braids, they cannot fall. They stay trapped inside the braid, mixed with a little product and a little lint, right at the base.

Think of each braid like a pocket in a coat. All season, small things collect in the pocket: coins, lint, a folded receipt. You do not notice them one by one. Then one day you turn the pocket inside out, and out comes a pile that looks huge. The pile did not grow in the pocket. It just had nowhere else to go. Shed hair in a braid is the same. The pile at takedown is weeks of normal days, all shown to you at once.

That is the loop this post keeps coming back to: the pile is normal, but the way you clear it decides what else comes out with it. Pull fast on dry hair, and the knot at the base tightens like a knot in a shoelace. The shed hair locks arms with the growing hair, and the force that frees the knot breaks the live strands in it. Go slow on wet, slippery hair, and the same knot slides apart with your fingers. Same head. Same pile. Two very different endings. The five steps below are built to give you the second ending.

What do you need on the towel before you start?

Lay out the tools first, the way a cook lays out pans before the heat is on. You need small scissors, a spray bottle filled with water and a good squeeze of conditioner mixed in, a wide-tooth comb, which is a comb with thick teeth set far apart, a few clips to hold sections, and a towel for your shoulders. A rat-tail comb, which is a comb with a long thin pointed handle, can help lift a braid at the very end of a section, but your fingers do most of this job.

What you do not need matters too. Put the fine-tooth comb away. Its teeth are close together, and close teeth catch on shed hair and drag. Put the clock away as well, as much as you can. A takedown for small or long braids can take hours, and many people split it over two days. That is not a flaw in the plan. That is the plan. Tired hands pull. Rested hands pick apart. If you feel your patience go thin at hour two, clip up what is done, twist it out of the way, and come back after a meal. The braids will wait. Your edges will thank you.

One more prep step: check your light. You will make your most important choice — where to cut — by sight, braid after braid. Sit where the light is bright and even, with the mirror close. A dim room is how good hair gets cut by mistake.

Step 1: How do you set up so the job stays calm?

Work in sections. Part your hair into four to six big sections and clip each one up, the way the sections were made on install day. Do the back and the middle first, and save the hairline and the nape for when you are fresh or for day two — no, wait. Let us say that the right way round, because it matters. The edges, which are the soft fine hairs along your hairline, are the weakest hair on your head. Do them when your hands are at their most patient, not at the end when you are rushing to be done. Many stylists do the edges first, with fresh hands and full light, and let the strong middle sections take the tired hours. Pick that order.

Set a simple rule for the whole job: one braid in your hands at a time. Not a handful. Not three at once to save time. One. Each braid gets cut, wet, unraveled, and cleared before the next one is touched. It sounds slow. It is, in fact, the fast way, because a section finished clean never has to be done twice, and tangles never get the chance to join up across braids into one big mat.

Step 2: Where do you cut, and where do you never cut?

Cut each braid below the point where your own hair ends inside it. Your hair ends somewhere along the braid; past that point, the braid is added hair only. Feel for it. Your own hair and the added hair often feel a bit different, and on many installs you can see where the thickness changes. Cut well below that line — lower than you think you need to. Here is the math that should guide your hand: a half inch of added hair left on costs you about two minutes of extra unraveling. An inch of your own hair cut by mistake costs you months of growing it back. When in doubt, cut lower. There is no prize for a close cut.

Never cut into a knot, and never cut at the root to save time. If a braid is sealed or melted at the end, cut the sealed tip off and unravel from there. If you cannot tell where your hair ends — on very long hair worn in very long braids, this can be hard — do not guess with scissors at all. Unravel that braid from the end by hand, no cut, until your own hair shows itself. Slow is safe. A guess is not.

Step 3: Why does each braid get wet before it gets opened?

Slip is the whole game. Slip is the slide one strand has against another when conditioner is on it. Dry shed hair grips. Wet, conditioned shed hair slides. Before you open a braid, spray it with your water and conditioner mix until it is damp from the end to the root, and let it sit for a minute while you cut the next one. Some people add a few drops of a light hair oil to their fingertips as well. The point is the same: nothing in this job should have to be forced apart dry.

Think of it like a wet knot in a rope versus a dry one. A dry knot pulled tight sets like stone. A wet knot, worked with your fingers, loosens a little with each pass. Hair at takedown is that rope. Water and conditioner do not just make it feel softer. They change what your hands are able to do.

Step 4: How do you unravel and clear the shed hair without breaking yours?

Start at the end of the damp braid and separate its strands with your fingers, working up toward the root a little at a time. When you reach the base, you will meet the knot: the packed mix of shed hair, product, and lint that sat at the root for weeks. Do not comb it. Do not pull it down in one motion. Rub it gently between your fingers, add more of your spray mix, and pick it apart from the bottom edge up, letting the shed hair slide out in small bits. The shed hair wants to leave; it is no longer attached at any root. Your job is to give it a wet, slippery path out that does not drag the attached hair with it.

Only when a section is fully open and the shed hair is out do you bring in the wide-tooth comb, and you use it the way you would on a child: start at the very ends, take short strokes, and work up an inch at a time, holding the section above the comb with your other hand so the pull lands in your hand and not on your scalp. Then twist that finished section loosely and clip it away, so it cannot tangle with the next one. Braid by braid, section by section, the head gets done. This is the turn in the whole job, the point where most people either keep their length or lose it: the comb comes last, after the fingers, never first.

Checklist:

  • Small scissors — for cutting added hair only, always below where your own hair ends
  • Spray bottle with water and conditioner mixed — for slip on every braid, before it is opened
  • Wide-tooth comb — used last in each section, ends first, never on the root knot
  • Clips and a towel — sections stay apart, finished hair stays finished
  • Your fingers — the main tool; if a knot will not move for fingers and spray, it is not ready for a comb
  • A trash bag within reach — shed hair goes in as you go, so the pile never scares you mid-job

Step 5: What do you do in the first wash and the days after?

When the last braid is out, do not pile all your hair on top of your head and scrub. Wash in the same sections you worked in, clipped apart, with a gentle shampoo — a shampoo made to clean hair and scalp without harsh stripping — and lukewarm water. Wash each section, rinse it, and clip it back. Then deep condition, which means leaving a rich conditioner on the hair for a set time so it can soften and strengthen the strands, and detangle once more with the conditioner in, ends first. Rinse, and squeeze the water out with a soft towel or an old cotton shirt rather than rubbing, since rubbing roughs up the outer layer of the strand you just spent hours protecting.

Then comes the step people skip, and it is the one your next style depends on: rest. Hair that has been braided for six to eight weeks has been held in one shape under light tension the whole time. Give it some days loose, or in a soft, low-tension style, before a new install pulls on the same roots again. If your scalp is sore, if you see small bumps along the hairline, broken hairs at the temples, or a spot that looks thin, that is a sign to pause and let a stylist look in person — and if a thin spot grows or does not fill back in, a skin doctor is the right next call, because hair loss caught early is the kind that responds best. That is not a scare line. It is the honest edge of a home guide: a takedown you can do; a diagnosis you should not guess at.

Here is the cost beat, plainly. The price of a careful takedown is time — an afternoon, sometimes a weekend — and the price of a rushed one is paid in length, edges, and a longer road back to the style you wanted next. Time is the cheaper bill, every time. You already spent weeks growing what is on your head. The takedown is where you decide whether you keep it.

What is the 1 DIY check that tells shed hair from breakage?

Run it over the trash bag before you throw it away. Pick up a few strands from the pile and look at the ends. A shed hair is full length, and at one end it often has a tiny white bulb — the small root tip that let go when the hair finished its growth cycle. A broken hair is short, has no bulb, and its end looks blunt or frayed, like a snapped thread. A pile of long, bulb-ended strands after seven weeks is your normal daily shed, saved up and shown to you all at once. A pile with lots of short, bulb-free bits is a warning that something in the wear or the takedown is snapping strands, and it is worth changing — looser installs, an earlier takedown date, or a stylist's eyes on your edges — before the next round. Check a few strands, not the whole bag. The story of the pile is written on the ends.

The bottom line

A braid takedown is not the chore after the style. It is the last step of the style itself, and it is where the growth you protected for weeks is either kept or lost. Cut low, work wet, fingers before comb, one braid at a time, wash in sections, and rest before the next install. Run the bulb check on the pile before you judge it. Do those things, and you will stand at that same Miramar mirror with a full head of your own hair in your hands — which was the whole point of braiding it in the first place.
